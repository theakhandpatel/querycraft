# Repeat purchase and cohort retention

Eight customers had bought twice by the end of March, and the March report counts six. The other two did not place an order in March, so the report leaves them out, and both figures are correct. That gap is the first thing to notice: "repeat by March" and "repeat and active in March" are different questions.

A customer is not repeat when they sign up. They become repeat when a second completed order lands, so the answer depends on the month you ask about. This chapter builds two reports from the same 37 completed orders. One counts repeat customers month by month. The other holds a group of customers fixed at their first purchase month and shows how many came back. The two reports answer different questions, and the difference between them is one word.

## Repeat is a status, and the grain is one customer per month

Take Ada Whitfield, customer 1. Her completed orders are 1001 and 1002, both placed in January 2026. On 31 January she had two completed orders, so from that date she is a repeat customer. In December 2025 she had none. The same person is not repeat and then is repeat, and nothing about her record changed except the date you read it.

That is why one flag per customer cannot answer the question. A boolean stored on the `customers` row forces you to choose a single date to evaluate it, and a report called "repeat customers" rarely says which date that was. The number is then correct for one moment and wrong for every other. That is the cost: a stored flag has no room to say when it was measured. The reader supplies the date, and the report never shows it.

Two words need separating before any query runs.

Repeat means the customer has at least two completed orders on or before the last day of the month in question. It is measured over the whole customer base, with no starting group.

Retained means the customer belongs to a stated starting group, called a cohort, and bought again in a later month. Retention always names where the customer started. Repeat does not.

Both definitions describe a person at a moment, so the next question is what one row of the report should stand for. That is the grain.

`orders` has order grain: one row, one order, 80 of them. `customers` has customer grain: one row, one person, 30 of them.

A count only means something next to the grain it counted. `COUNT(*)` over `orders` answers how many orders there were. If the question is how many people ordered, `COUNT(*)` answers the wrong one. Ada has three completed orders, so she contributes three rows to a query over `orders` and one row to a query over `customers`.

The grain this chapter uses is one row per customer per month. Each row says this person was repeat in this month. A customer appears once per month in which they qualify, and not at all in months where they do not.

That grain has three properties worth knowing. It survives new data, because an order placed today changes the months after it and leaves the earlier months alone. It shows the trend, because every month gets its own count. And it is checkable, because the number of repeat customers in a month can never exceed the number of customers who ordered in that month.

## A running count marks the moment a customer turns repeat

A running count grows as you read down the rows. You get one by giving `COUNT(*)` a window with an `ORDER BY` inside it. That frame means the rows from the start of the partition to the current row, so a row's count includes itself (module 5 covers frames).

```sql
SELECT customer_id, id AS order_id,
       DATE_TRUNC('month', ordered_at)::date AS month,
       COUNT(*) OVER (
         PARTITION BY customer_id
         ORDER BY ordered_at, id
       ) AS orders_so_far
FROM orders
WHERE status = 'completed'
ORDER BY customer_id, ordered_at, id;
```

The first three customers come out like this.

| customer_id | order_id | month | orders_so_far |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 1001 | 2026-01-01 | 1 |
| 1 | 1002 | 2026-01-01 | 2 |
| 1 | 1005 | 2026-02-01 | 3 |
| 2 | 1008 | 2026-01-01 | 1 |
| 3 | 1009 | 2026-01-01 | 1 |
| 3 | 1010 | 2026-01-01 | 2 |
| 3 | 1004 | 2026-02-01 | 3 |

`PARTITION BY customer_id` restarts the count for each customer, so no count mixes two people. `ORDER BY ordered_at, id` inside the window sets the sequence the count follows. The trailing `id` breaks ties: two orders sharing a timestamp have no defined order, and without the tie-break the row where the count reaches 2 could move.

Read customer 3 down the table. Her count goes 1, 2, 3 across three rows in two months. The row with `orders_so_far = 2` is the moment she turns repeat, and every later row keeps that flag true. Customer 2 has one completed order, so her count stops at 1 and she never appears in a repeat report.

The flag is `orders_so_far >= 2`, and it cannot go in the `WHERE` clause of the query that produces it. `WHERE` runs before window functions, so the count does not exist yet. Put the window in a CTE and filter outside it.

## Repeat customers per month

```sql
WITH history AS (
    SELECT customer_id,
           DATE_TRUNC('month', ordered_at)::date AS month,
           COUNT(*) OVER (
             PARTITION BY customer_id
             ORDER BY ordered_at, id
           ) AS orders_so_far
    FROM orders
    WHERE status = 'completed'
)
SELECT month,
       COUNT(DISTINCT customer_id) AS repeat_customers
FROM history
WHERE orders_so_far >= 2
GROUP BY month
ORDER BY month;
```

Four steps produce the answer.

`history` keeps the 37 completed orders and attaches `orders_so_far`. Its grain is one row per completed order.

The outer `WHERE orders_so_far >= 2` drops every row from before the customer's second order. What survives is one row per completed order placed by a customer who was already repeat at that point.

`GROUP BY month` collapses those rows to one per month.

`COUNT(DISTINCT customer_id)` counts the people in each month, not the rows that survived step two.

| month | repeat_customers |
| --- | --- |
| 2026-01-01 | 2 |
| 2026-02-01 | 2 |
| 2026-03-01 | 6 |
| 2026-04-01 | 5 |
| 2026-05-01 | 3 |

January reads 2 because only two customers had reached two completed orders by 31 January, and both placed another completed order during the month. March reads 6.

Read the shape with care. The figure can fall, and it does between March and April, because buying in the month is part of the condition. A customer counts here only if they were already repeat and also placed a completed order that month. This is not a cumulative count of everyone who ever became repeat; it is the number of repeat customers who were active that month.

The January figure is also worth holding beside the January cohort. Six customers had their first completed order in January, and only two of them were repeat by the end of it. New and repeat measure different things and can sit in the same month without contradicting each other.

## A cohort is a group fixed at first purchase

A cohort is a set of people who share a first event. Here the event is a first completed order, and the cohort key is the month it happened in.

The key is fixed when the customer first appears and never changes as later orders arrive. That is the whole point. A group defined as "customers who bought in March" gains and loses members as people return or stop, so two months' figures for that group compare different people. A group defined by first purchase holds still, so a later month's count is genuinely about the same people.

The cohort month comes from one aggregate per customer:

```sql
SELECT customer_id,
       MIN(DATE_TRUNC('month', ordered_at))::date AS cohort_month
FROM orders
WHERE status = 'completed'
GROUP BY customer_id;
```

That returns 19 rows, one per customer with a completed order, and the cohort sizes are these.

| cohort_month | customers |
| --- | --- |
| 2026-01-01 | 6 |
| 2026-02-01 | 6 |
| 2026-03-01 | 3 |
| 2026-04-01 | 2 |
| 2026-05-01 | 2 |

The column sums to 19, which matches `COUNT(DISTINCT customer_id)` over completed orders. That check is worth running after any cohort query, because a join that fans out one side will break it.

When you need another column from that first order, `DISTINCT ON (customer_id)` with `ORDER BY customer_id, ordered_at, id` keeps the first row per customer and returns the whole row. `ROW_NUMBER() = 1` over the same ordering does the same job inside a CTE. One row per customer is the grain that makes the cohort key safe to join back to activity: nothing duplicates.

## Month index measures distance from the cohort month

Comparing cohorts needs time measured from each customer's own start. The month index is the number of calendar months between the cohort month and an activity month. Index 0 is the cohort month itself, index 1 the next month, and so on.

Both dates are already truncated to the first of their month. The exact difference is therefore a year part plus a month part.

```sql
(EXTRACT(YEAR FROM activity_month) - EXTRACT(YEAR FROM cohort_month)) * 12
  + (EXTRACT(MONTH FROM activity_month) - EXTRACT(MONTH FROM cohort_month))
```

Twelve months per year carries the year difference into the month count, and the month difference adds the rest. No rounding, no assumption about month length.

The tempting shortcut subtracts the dates and divides by 30, since subtracting two dates yields an integer day count.

```sql
-- wrong: a calendar month is not 30 days
((activity_month - cohort_month) / 30)::int AS month_index
```

For the January cohort that shortcut returns 6 customers at index 0, 4 at index 1, and 1 at index 3. The correct grid is 6 at index 0, 2 at index 1, 2 at index 2, and 1 at index 3. February and March both collapsed into index 1, because 1 January to 1 March is 59 days and integer division turns that into 1. Index 2 never appears, so one row of the grid vanished and another doubled.

Try this: in the grid query below, swap the calendar arithmetic for the `/ 30` line and re-run it. The two dates there are `a.month` and `f.cohort_month`. The January cohort reads 6 at index 0, 4 at index 1 and 1 at index 3. The 2 customers active in February and the 2 active in March both land on index 1, so two rows of the report fold into one.

Months run from 28 to 31 days, so any fixed divisor drifts. The year and month arithmetic has no such failure because it never leaves the calendar.

## The cohort grid, and the months it leaves out

The two pieces join into one report: the cohort key on the left, and the activity months with their index on the right. This is the retention report, and its rows are the retained counts: of the customers who started in that month, how many were active at each distance from it.

```sql
WITH first_order AS (
    SELECT customer_id,
           MIN(DATE_TRUNC('month', ordered_at))::date AS cohort_month
    FROM orders
    WHERE status = 'completed'
    GROUP BY customer_id
),
activity AS (
    SELECT customer_id,
           DATE_TRUNC('month', ordered_at)::date AS month
    FROM orders
    WHERE status = 'completed'
    GROUP BY 1, 2
)
SELECT f.cohort_month,
       ( (EXTRACT(YEAR FROM a.month) - EXTRACT(YEAR FROM f.cohort_month)) * 12
       + (EXTRACT(MONTH FROM a.month) - EXTRACT(MONTH FROM f.cohort_month)) )::int AS month_index,
       COUNT(DISTINCT a.customer_id) AS active_customers
FROM first_order f
JOIN activity a ON a.customer_id = f.customer_id
GROUP BY 1, 2
ORDER BY 1, 2;
```

| cohort_month | month_index | active_customers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2026-01-01 | 0 | 6 |
| 2026-01-01 | 1 | 2 |
| 2026-01-01 | 2 | 2 |
| 2026-01-01 | 3 | 1 |
| 2026-02-01 | 0 | 6 |
| 2026-02-01 | 1 | 4 |
| 2026-02-01 | 2 | 2 |
| 2026-03-01 | 0 | 3 |
| 2026-03-01 | 1 | 2 |
| 2026-03-01 | 2 | 2 |
| 2026-04-01 | 0 | 2 |
| 2026-04-01 | 1 | 1 |
| 2026-05-01 | 0 | 2 |

Read one cohort across. The six customers who started in January were all active in January, two came back in February, two in March, and one in April. Read one column down. Index 0 gives 6, 6, 3, 2 and 2, which sums to 19, the size of the whole active base.

Some cells are missing on purpose, for two different reasons. The February cohort has no index 3 row even though May exists, so none of its six customers bought in May. That is a real zero. The March cohort has no index 3 row and the April cohort none at index 2, because those slots would be June, past the last month in the data.

In all three cases the query had no row to group: `GROUP BY` forms a group only from rows that exist, and `COUNT` never returns 0 without a row to stand on.

The output cannot tell a real zero from a month that has not happened yet, because both print nothing. That distinction bites in two places. A line chart drawn straight from this output shows a gap rather than a zero. An average over the visible rows is computed over a different set of months for each cohort. The fix is to generate the missing months first and `LEFT JOIN` the activity to them, which is what module 9 builds with a recursive CTE.

## Why `COUNT(DISTINCT customer_id)` matters

Drop the `activity` CTE and join the orders straight to the `first_order` key from the grid above. The report then gives the wrong number in the first cell.

```sql
-- wrong: counts orders, not customers
SELECT f.cohort_month,
       DATE_TRUNC('month', o.ordered_at)::date AS activity_month,
       COUNT(*) AS active_customers
FROM orders o
JOIN first_order f ON f.customer_id = o.customer_id
WHERE o.status = 'completed'
GROUP BY 1, 2
ORDER BY 1, 2;
```

| cohort_month | activity_month | count of rows | count of customers |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2026-01-01 | 2026-01-01 | 8 | 6 |
| 2026-01-01 | 2026-02-01 | 2 | 2 |
| 2026-01-01 | 2026-03-01 | 2 | 2 |
| 2026-01-01 | 2026-04-01 | 1 | 1 |

Six January customers placed eight completed orders that month, so `COUNT(*)` counted the orders. The two extra rows are repeat purchases by people already in the count. The other cells shown here are unaffected, because each of those customers ordered once in that month. That is what makes the bug expensive. It appears only in the cells where somebody bought twice, so a glance at one column can miss it.

There are two clean fixes, and the chapter uses both. `COUNT(DISTINCT o.customer_id)` counts a person once per group however many rows they occupy. Pre-aggregating activity to one row per customer per month, as the `activity` CTE does, keeps the duplicate row from existing, so `COUNT(*)` is then safe on that result.

The rule that covers both: after a `JOIN`, decide what one row of the joined result stands for before you count anything. If the answer is "one order", then a count of orders is correct and a count of customers needs `DISTINCT`. If the answer is "one customer", the counting is already safe and the duplicate came from somewhere else.

## What to remember

**What I would do**: compute repeat in the query, one row per customer per month, and keep the flag off the `customers` row. Name the grain in the CTE so the next reader knows what a row stands for. Check the cohort sizes against `COUNT(DISTINCT customer_id)` before trusting a single cell. When a month looks empty, ask whether it is a real zero or a month that never had a row.

- Repeat is a status as of a month, not a property of a customer. Say which month before you report the number.
- Grain is what one row stands for. Every count in this chapter either counts orders or counts people, and the query has to choose.
- A `COUNT(*)` window with `ORDER BY ordered_at, id` marks the row where a customer turns repeat, and the unique `id` makes that row deterministic.
- A cohort key comes from the first event and never changes, so a later month's figure describes the same people.
- Month index is calendar arithmetic on truncated dates, not day counts divided by 30.
- A month with no rows produces no output row, so a dense grid needs a generated series and a `LEFT JOIN`.

## Check yourself

**1. Why is "repeat" a per-customer-month fact rather than one fixed fact about a customer?**

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Because it depends on what had happened by a given month. A customer becomes repeat when their second completed order lands, so as of January they may not be repeat while as of March they are. Storing one flag per customer forces a date to be chosen, and computing it per month keeps the status honest.

</details>

**2. Why does the monthly report need `COUNT(DISTINCT customer_id)` rather than `COUNT(*)`?**

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Because one customer can hold several completed orders in a month, and a join can multiply their rows further. `COUNT(*)` counts those rows; `DISTINCT` counts the person once per month, which is what a count of customers means.

</details>

**3. What does a retention grid need that a repeat count does not?**

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

A cohort and a month index. Retention measures a fixed starting group at a month relative to its first purchase. Repeat status can be computed for the whole customer base with no cohort at all, which makes retained the stricter word.

</details>

**4. The February cohort has no row at month index 3, which is May. Does that mean none of its customers bought in May?**

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Yes, for that cohort. May had completed orders, but none from a customer whose first purchase was in February, and a month with no qualifying rows produces no group. The same absent row appears for months past the end of the data, where the answer is unknown rather than zero. A generated month series joined with `LEFT JOIN` makes both cases visible as a 0.

</details>

**5. Why does dividing by 30 break the January cohort grid?**

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Because month lengths run from 28 to 31 days. Integer division by 30 maps February and March onto the same index and drops index 2. The year and month arithmetic is exact because both dates are already the first of their month.

</details>
